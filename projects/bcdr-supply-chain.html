The Clock Starts Before You Know What Happened

A business continuity and disaster recovery plan for a cloud data-processing provider hit by a SolarWinds-style supply chain compromise. I owned the incident command structure and the system architecture. The thing that reshaped how I think about incident response: in Australian critical infrastructure, the statutory notification clock starts long before anyone knows the scope of the breach.

00 / Scope

Group coursework, completed and assessed. The document attributed sections by author; mine were Roles and Responsibilities, System Architecture, and Conclusions. This page covers only those, and describes my reasoning rather than reproducing the submitted document. My teammates' work isn't mine to publish, so it isn't described here and they aren't named. The scenario company and incident are fictional.

01 / The brief

An attack that arrives through the front door, signed

The scenario: a provider of cloud data storage and processing for enterprise and government clients, compromised the way SolarWinds was — malicious code inserted into a legitimate software update from a trusted vendor. The defining property of a supply chain attack is that every perimeter control performs correctly. The update is signed, the vendor is legitimate, the traffic is expected. Nothing is bypassed, because nothing needs to be.

That makes it an unusually good teaching scenario, because it strips away the comfort of assuming that prevention works. The plan has to answer what you do when the thing you trusted is the thing that got you.

02 / Roles and responsibilities

The org chart is shaped by law, not convenience

A conventional roles table covers who investigates, who contains and who restores. That isn't enough here, because in Australian critical infrastructure the regulatory clocks start at declaration — not at the point you understand what happened.

12 hr

SOCI Act critical incident report

Filed with the Cyber and Infrastructure Security Centre while containment is still underway.

72 hr

NDB assessment commences

Under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, leading to OAIC notification where personal information is involved.

CPS 234

APRA-regulated clients

Downstream notification obligations that land on customers, not just on us.

A 12-hour report means someone files while the investigation is still running. So the structure has to answer "who is authorised to declare, and who owns the clock" before it answers "who fixes the server."

That produced a deliberate separation. The CISO acts as Incident Commander and declares — because declaration is what starts every clock, and it can't wait on consensus. A separate Legal and Compliance role owns the regulatory timeline independently, so statutory deadlines aren't competing for the incident commander's attention during containment. Executive authority for disaster declaration, failover, and public notification sits with the CEO, since under the NDB scheme accountability for meeting notification obligations is ultimately theirs.

One detail I'd defend: evidence handling is a first-class responsibility, not a by-product of investigation. Containment instincts — reimage, restore, move on — destroy exactly what a regulator later asks for. Assigning chain of custody explicitly to the incident response lead makes forensic preservation someone's job rather than something everyone assumes someone else did.

03 / System architecture

Four zones, and an enforcement point in the middle

The architecture is defence-in-depth, but arranged around the specific failure mode: the threat actor never touches the perimeter, because they compromise the update server upstream of it.

01

External perimeter

Router, external firewall and DMZ filtering public traffic to the web portal — correct, and irrelevant to this attack.

02

Enforcement layer

Zero Trust policy intercepting all traffic including trusted updates, with SIEM aggregation, IDS, and a key vault as cryptographic authority.

03

IT system

Approved traffic only, through an internal firewall to CI/CD, application and database servers, with warm-site failover at a 15-minute RPO.

04

OT system

Controllers and field devices behind a dedicated OT firewall, telemetry flowing one way back to the enforcement layer.

Putting the Zero Trust policy point between the update source and everything else is the whole argument. A perimeter model asks whether traffic came from outside. This asks whether a signed, expected update from a trusted vendor should be allowed to execute — which is the only question that would have caught the scenario.

The one-way OT telemetry is the part I'd most want to explain. Industrial controllers are the same problem class as the unpatchable medical devices in my thesis work: long-lived, hard to patch, and unable to host an agent. You can observe them or you can control them, and observing them through a one-way path means the monitoring channel can't become an attack path into the plant. It costs you the ability to remediate remotely. That's a trade I'd make in OT and wouldn't make in IT.

04 / A fuller version

What it would add

The plan opens with a list of preconditions — signed artefacts, network segmentation, a provenance-based IDS integrated with the SIEM, an operational warm site with active replication, trained staff. Each one is reasonable. Together they describe an organisation that has already done years of security work.

  • Sequenced preconditions. A real organisation has none of them on day one. The more useful version sequences them — what you do in year one with none of this in place — rather than listing them as given.
  • A costed recovery objective. A 15-minute RPO implies continuous replication to a geographically separate site, one of the more expensive commitments in any plan. A fuller version would justify it against cheaper tiers rather than assert it.
  • Named deputies. A command structure that works at 2pm on a Tuesday also needs deputies for a declaration at 3am on a public holiday.
05 / What it connects to

The response path

Working through my governance analysis of clinical IoMT, the sharpest gap I found was that a detector without a response path is an alarm nobody is required to answer. This is the other half of that: who declares, who is accountable, which clocks are running, and what the architecture has to look like for containment to be possible at all.
