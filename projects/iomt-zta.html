Zero-Trust Anomaly Detection for Clinical IoMT

An end-to-end machine-learning pipeline that detects anomalous network traffic on medical IoT devices and feeds that signal into Zero Trust policy decisions — built under a hard constraint most anomaly-detection research sets aside: it has to run on hardware a hospital already owns.

01 / Problem

Medical devices you can't patch, on networks that trust them

Infusion pumps, patient monitors and imaging equipment sit on hospital networks for a decade or more. They often run firmware that can't be patched on a normal cycle, can't host an endpoint agent, and were designed in an era that assumed the network itself was safe. In a flat clinical network, one compromised device is a foothold with a clear path to everything else.

Zero Trust Architecture is the obvious answer — verify every request, trust no device implicitly. But ZTA needs a live signal about whether a device is behaving normally, and that signal has to come from somewhere. For devices that can't run an agent, the only thing left to observe is their network traffic.

02 / Constraint

The overhead nobody budgets for

Most published IoMT anomaly detection reports accuracy on a workstation or a GPU. That's a fair benchmark for the model and a poor one for the deployment. A clinical network segment doesn't come with spare compute, and adding a detection layer that needs its own server is a procurement problem before it's ever a security one.

The research question isn't "can machine learning detect this?" — it's whether detection survives being shrunk to fit the hardware that's actually available at the edge.

So computational overhead is treated as a first-class constraint here rather than an afterthought: lightweight models, aggressive feature selection, and benchmarking on constrained hardware rather than only on the development machine.

03 / Approach

Three components, one decision loop

The pipeline is built around a clean separation: detect the anomaly, convert it into a trust score that behaves sensibly over time, then let policy act on that score with awareness of what kind of device it's looking at.

01

Anomaly detector

A Random Forest classifier over network-flow features.

02

Trust engine

Converts detections into a continuous trust score using asymmetric decay and EMA smoothing — trust drops fast on evidence of compromise and recovers slowly.

03

Policy enforcement

Device-class-conditioned rules, so the same trust score can mean different things for an infusion pump than for a monitor.

The asymmetric decay is the piece I'd defend hardest. Symmetric trust scoring lets a device that misbehaves intermittently keep climbing back to full trust between incidents — which is exactly the pattern a slow, careful attacker produces. Making recovery slower than penalty means a device has to demonstrate sustained normal behaviour before it's trusted again.

Evaluation runs across three feature-set configurations, so the accuracy/overhead trade-off is measured rather than assumed — the smallest configuration is the one that matters for edge deployment.

05 / What was hard

The parts that didn't go to plan

  • The dataset's structure limited what could be done directly. The attacks folder shipped without label or device_id columns, which had to be handled before training could proceed.
  • Feature selection didn't scale. Running selection and grid search over the full dataset was computationally impractical, so both were run on subsampled data.
  • A dtype bug in the simulation stage had to be found and fixed.
06 / Next

Where it goes from here

  • Extending overhead benchmarking across a wider range of edge hardware profiles.
  • Testing the trust engine's decay parameters against adversarial pacing — an attacker who deliberately spaces activity to stay under the recovery curve.
  • Evaluating how the policy layer behaves during legitimate device maintenance windows, which look anomalous but aren't.
